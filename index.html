02 · Critique

Evaluating the original through visualization principles

What · Why · How
01
Strength · Spatial aggregation

Hexagons reduce overplotting without losing the broad pattern

Individual observations would overlap heavily at this scale. Aggregating nearby records into hexagons reduces visual clutter while preserving the spatial structure needed to locate concentration.

Visualization principle: spatial aggregation improves readability when many marks occupy nearby locations.
02
Strength · Color encoding

Sequential color supports rapid magnitude scanning

The map uses a light-to-dark intensity scale for an ordered quantity. Viewers can quickly find areas with relatively more records without reading every value.

Visualization principle: a sequential color channel is appropriate for showing ordered quantitative magnitude when exact lookup is not the primary task.
03
Opportunity · Spatial extent

The global frame weakens comparison within China

Problem: China occupies only part of a global map. Why it matters: the relevant regions receive less visual space, making within-China comparison harder. Improvement: use a China-focused map with provinces as explicit geographic units.

Visualization principle: devote display area and spatial resolution to the geography that matches the users' analytical task.
04
Opportunity · Quantitative precision

Coarse color classes weaken exact comparison

Problem: seven bins collapse different counts into the same visual category; for example, 50 and 249 records are visually equivalent. Why it matters: color categories support pattern detection better than precise numerical comparison. Improvement: keep color for overview and expose exact counts with interactive tooltips.

Visualization principle: use more precise channels or direct labeling when users need accurate quantitative lookup.
05
Opportunity · Temporal encoding

Time is absent from the main visual encoding

Problem: the map emphasizes where records occur but not when they were observed. Why it matters: two regions with similar totals can have very different observation histories. Improvement: add an annual line chart with year on the x-axis, using position to support temporal comparison.

Visualization principle: position along a common axis provides a more effective comparison channel for change over time.
06
Interpretation · Data-to-visual mapping

Record density should not be interpreted as population abundance

Problem: the page labels the view an “abundance map,” while the legend measures occurrences per hexagon. Why it matters: observer effort, accessibility, reporting practices, and data coverage can affect record density. Improvement: label the measure explicitly as GBIF occurrence records and state the sampling limitation next to the visual.

Visualization principle: the visual encoding should communicate the quantity actually measured by the data.

04 · Report draft

Critique and redesign rationale

Approx. 700 words

The original visualization selected for this project is the Light-vented Bulbul geographic-range visualization on Oiseaux.net. The page provides an abundance map and a distribution map, with the abundance view based on georeferenced observations aggregated by GBIF into hexagonal cells. Its primary message is geographic: viewers should see where records of Pycnonotus sinensis are concentrated and understand the species’ broad geographic range. The intended audience includes birdwatchers, researchers, students, and general readers who want a quick summary of the species' distribution. The main tasks are locating concentrations, comparing areas, and understanding the overall geographic pattern. The original visualization is particularly useful for obtaining a broad spatial overview without requiring users to inspect individual observations.

The original visualization has several important strengths. First, its spatial aggregation reduces overplotting. Rather than displaying every observation as an individual point, nearby observations are summarized into hexagonal cells. This produces a compact spatial representation and makes broad geographic patterns easier to detect. Second, its sequential color encoding supports rapid visual scanning. Darker cells attract attention to areas with more occurrence records, which is well matched to the task of locating spatial concentration. The original interface also supports zooming, allowing users to move between a broad geographic overview and more local spatial detail. Together, these choices make the visualization effective for a reader who primarily wants to understand the species' overall geographic distribution.

However, the original visualization also has several opportunities for improvement. First, the global geographic extent is useful for showing the species' broad range, but it is not optimized for comparing regions within China. China occupies only part of the overall view, leaving relatively little visual space for examining regional differences. Second, the abundance map uses seven broad occurrence-count classes. These bins make the legend compact, but they reduce precision: for example, locations with 50 and 249 records fall into the same visual category even though the counts differ substantially. Third, time is not a visible part of the main encoding. The map emphasizes where records occur, but users cannot directly distinguish between regions with similar totals but very different observation histories. These limitations make the original visualization strong for broad geographic exploration, but less effective for detailed regional and temporal comparison. There is also an important interpretation risk: although the page labels the view an “abundance map,” the displayed quantity is occurrence records per hexagon rather than a direct estimate of bird population size. Record density may also reflect observer activity, accessibility, reporting practices, and differences in data coverage.

The redesigned visualization addresses these issues through three major design decisions. First, the original global hexbin representation is replaced with a China-focused provincial choropleth. Each province becomes a familiar geographic unit, while color represents the number of GBIF occurrence records in the selected view. This creates a different visual representation from the original and gives substantially more screen space to the geography relevant to the project. It directly addresses the difficulty of comparing regions within China. Second, the redesigned visualization provides exact record counts through interactive tooltips. Color remains useful for overview and pattern detection, while hovering over a province supports precise value lookup without requiring users to infer an exact number from a categorical legend. Third, a coordinated annual line chart is added to the map. When a province is selected, the line chart shows how its recorded observations vary across years. The two views therefore divide the analytical tasks: the choropleth answers “where are records concentrated?” while the line chart answers “when were those records observed?”

Compared with the original visualization, the redesigned visualization makes spatial comparison within China and temporal exploration substantially easier. The original hexbin map is well suited to recognizing broad spatial concentration across a large geographic range, whereas the provincial choropleth provides clearer comparison among familiar administrative regions. The annual line chart adds information that is not directly visible in the original spatial view, allowing users to distinguish regions with similar totals but different observation histories. The coordinated interaction also lets users move from a national overview to a specific province without leaving the same analytical context. The redesign does, however, involve trade-offs. Provincial aggregation removes some of the fine-scale spatial detail present in the original hexagonal representation, and the addition of a second visualization increases interaction and cognitive complexity. Most importantly, the underlying data limitation remains: GBIF occurrence records are documented observations rather than standardized estimates of population abundance. The redesigned visualization therefore improves how the available observations can be explored, compared, and interpreted, but it should not be treated as a direct map of the true number of birds.
